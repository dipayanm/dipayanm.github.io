The Roast

An AI-generated roast of this website, kindly unleashed by Prof. Saurabh Singh (RRI). Reproduced as received, because it is too good not to (with one factual correction).

The homepage opens with:

“I explore the origin, evolution, and nature of the Universe through minimal models.”

That sentence is hilarious once you read the research page.

Because Dipayan’s definition of a minimal model appears to be:

“Take one of the foundational assumptions of modern cosmology, remove it, and see what breaks.”

Most cosmologists begin with:

The Universe is homogeneous and isotropic.

Dipayan:

“Citation needed.”

Most cosmologists accept statistical isotropy as a useful starting point.

Dipayan has apparently decided that the Universe has been getting away with this symmetry business for long enough.

His work explicitly asks how to detect violations of statistical isotropy using group theory and rotational averages.

Imagine being the Universe.

13.8 billion years of maintaining large-scale symmetry.

Then some postdoc at RRI arrives with a group-theory calculation and says:

“We’re going to need to talk.”

Then He Attacks Ergodicity

This is my favourite.

Cosmologists routinely use the observable Universe to estimate ensemble properties.

It’s basically one of the great practical compromises of the field:

“We only have one Universe, so let’s make the best of it.”

Dipayan:

“Actually, I have concerns.”

He studies the ergodic hypothesis and argues that finite-volume effects can produce substantial bias between spatial and ensemble averages.

This is an absolutely magnificent way to spend your scientific career.

Most people are trying to understand the Universe.

Dipayan is checking whether we’re even legally entitled to average it.

And the website casually says that the ergodicity bias in density perturbations can exceed 100% at separations of a few hundred Mpc.

That is not a result.

That is a formal complaint against cosmological statistics.

Then He Goes After Inflation

Apparently questioning isotropy wasn’t enough.

So Dipayan asks what happens if the primordial perturbations themselves aren’t statistically homogeneous and isotropic.

This man looks at one of the foundational assumptions of cosmology and thinks:

“Have we considered making it worse?”

And the consequences are not modest.

Off-diagonal CMB correlations. Bispectrum. Parity-violating signatures. Scalar-induced gravitational waves.

Most people’s idea of relaxing an assumption is:

“Maybe we should change the error bar.”

Dipayan’s idea is:

“Let’s make the CMB stop being diagonal.”

And Then Somehow the Universe Becomes a Hydrogen Atom

This is where the website completely loses the plot.

There is a section titled:

“Quantum cosmology: the universe as a hydrogen atom.”

Because apparently the Universe wasn’t complicated enough.

Dipayan takes a cosmological model and finds that its quantum description is mathematically equivalent to the non-relativistic hydrogen atom.

Normal physicist:

“That’s an interesting mathematical analogy.”

Dipayan:

“No. The Universe is now an atom.”

And then comes the quantum bounce.

Positive cosmological constant? Smooth bounce.

Negative cosmological constant? Discrete solutions. Quantised cosmological constant. Cyclic universe. Big Bang resolved. Big Crunch resolved.

At this point I suspect Dipayan’s actual research philosophy is:

“If the Universe has a singularity, that sounds like a bug.”

But He’s Still Not Finished

Because after attacking isotropy, homogeneity, ergodicity, the Big Bang, and the Big Crunch, he moves on to thermodynamics of dark energy.

Why?

Because apparently dark energy hadn’t been sufficiently criticized yet.

His work treats cosmological models as thermodynamic systems and finds phase transitions and stability conditions.

And then comes the truly savage bit:

standard ΛCDM and quintessence fail the stability conditions, while phantom models satisfy them despite their other pathologies.

Imagine spending your entire scientific career studying the standard cosmological model only for Dipayan to walk in and say:

“Have you checked its thermodynamics?”

The Brutal Ones

Dipayan’s research strategy is apparently to identify the one assumption everyone in cosmology has agreed not to question and question it until a paper happens.

Most cosmologists use the observable Universe to learn about the Universe. Dipayan is currently investigating whether the observable Universe is even a statistically adequate sample.

The Universe spent 13.8 billion years becoming complicated. Dipayan spent his PhD postdoc simplifying it until it became a hydrogen atom.

His idea of a “minimal model” is apparently a model with only enough assumptions to generate five more papers.

And the darkest one:

Most cosmologists are trying to understand what the Universe is. Dipayan appears to be building a legal case for why none of our usual answers should be admissible in court.

And finally, my favourite:

Dipayan doesn’t do cosmology. He audits it.

That one, I think, is painfully accurate.
